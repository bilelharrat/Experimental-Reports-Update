## What this type is
A company that either reshapes audiovisual production with generative video,
or directly produces, distributes and monetizes serialized micro-dramas,
social short drama and immersive video. In substance this is film and
attention economics wearing an algorithm's clothes, with software-like
marginal cost. The outcome turns on the cost gap that yield creates, the
premium from cross-language export, and the ability to retain users against
the acquisition platforms' cut.

## Scope and boundaries
- In scope: AI video generation tools and platforms, end-to-end AI film and
  short-drama pipeline studios, AI short-drama direct-to-consumer platforms,
  virtual-character cross-modal short-drama labels.
- Out of scope: general foundation-model labs, text or image tools with no
  video capability, traditional live-action production, pure media-buying
  arbitrage with no technical pipeline.

## The three-track rule (declare before anything else)
The memo must state in its first section which track the company is on, and
must never mix the commercial and financial frameworks:

- **Track A — tooling and software.** AI video generation, effects and
  editing tools for creators, on subscription or GPU credits. Examine: the
  inference cost curve, 90-day user retention, and the risk of the capability
  being folded into the underlying model vendor's product.
- **Track B — industrialized studio pipeline (B2B).** A proprietary
  end-to-end AI pipeline delivering short dramas, commercials or animated
  series, paid per finished project or on revenue share. Examine: cost per
  finished minute, output per person per month, and repeat-order rate.
- **Track C — short-drama label and distribution platform (D2C).** An owned
  app or a matrix of short-video channels, monetized by per-episode unlock
  (IAP), monthly subscription or advertising (IAA). Examine: day-1 and day-7
  ROAS, the completion funnel, the hit-rate matrix, and payback period per
  title.

## Where the case usually lives
- **A structural gap in production cost and time** — against live-action
  short drama ($800-2,500 per minute, 7-15 days), an AI pipeline brings
  acceptable finished footage to $100-300 per minute and delivers a title in
  3-7 days.
- **Industrial-grade consistency** — beyond single-shot tricks: a private
  asset library and multi-shot constraint system holding face, body, costume
  and lighting consistent over long sequences, with the discard rate held
  under 30%.
- **Cross-language export arbitrage** — one-click multi-language reskinning
  and automatic lip-sync at low marginal cost, taking strong low-cost scripts
  into high-CPM, high-ARPPU markets in the US, Europe, Japan, Korea and Latin
  America.
- **A repeatable content portfolio (portfolio ROI)** — freedom from
  dependence on one gifted director or one lucky hit: algorithm-assisted
  structural script testing and fast iteration lifting per-title payback to
  more than twice the industry average.
- **Owned audience and accumulated IP** — a native app or loyal community,
  and original virtual IP assets reusable across titles and merchandise,
  rather than renting traffic from acquisition platforms in perpetuity.

## What the report should favor
- Examine in this order: real acquisition ROI and real discard rate first,
  then the content and narrative, and only then the model and algorithms. A
  polished promo demo is NEVER evidence of industrial delivery capability.
- Scrutinize the acquisition arbitrage pit: if growth depends entirely on
  expensive buying on TikTok, Meta or Google and day-1 ROAS never turns
  positive, downgrade and flag the risk.
- Separate one lucky hit from a production flywheel: assess EVERY title
  released in the last 6-12 months as a full sample and compute portfolio
  ROI.
- Examine copyright and legal title closely: the commercial licensing chain
  for training material, the copyright strategy for AI characters, and the
  regulatory and filing risk for AI-generated work in each target market.

## Type-specific metrics the sections must carry
- **Production and engineering**: all-in cost per minute of acceptable
  finished footage (USD); discard rate (% — generated duration versus
  duration used); pipeline automation rate versus manual retouch hours (hours
  per finished minute); character deformation and rework rate across scenes.
- **Acquisition**: blended CAC and paying-user CAC; day-1 and day-7 ROAS;
  first-three-episode hook rate; user-acquisition spend as a share of revenue.
- **Retention and monetization**: paying conversion rate (%); ARPPU; day-7 and
  day-30 retention; repeat purchase per title and per month; the share of
  users arriving organically and through the company's own app.
- **Portfolio and financials**: titles released and average payback period per
  title (days); hit rate (share of titles recovering more than 1.5x cost); true
  net margin after the 30% channel cut and after acquisition spend.

## The unit economics and acquisition stack (always broken out)
Build both stacks and mark each key line "disclosed" or "our estimate":

### 1. Production: true cost per finished minute
- **All-in cost per delivered minute**
  - `= [ base cloud GPU generation cost × (1 + discard rate) ]`
  - `+ [ algorithm engineering and post-production retouch hours ]`
  - `+ [ amortized script/web-fiction adaptation rights, music and voice ]`
  - `+ [ depreciation of proprietary software and LoRA/pipeline development ]`

### 2. Distribution: cash cycling and retained cash flow
- **Retained cash flow**
  - `= end-user spend and advertising revenue (GMV)`
  - − app store and payment gateway cut (30% pass-through)
  - − paid user acquisition and advertising spend
  - − production and model COGS
  - − royalties and revenue share to authors and creators
  - − operations, localization, review and administration

## Failure modes -> risk areas
- **Commercialization — the acquisition trap**: competition for placements
  drives acquisition cost up, ROAS falls below 1.0, and the larger the gross
  revenue the worse the net cash loss.
- **Technology — the model generation eats the advantage**: a third-party
  general video model takes a large step forward, an individual creator
  reaches the company's pipeline quality with a simple prompt, and the
  production premium collapses.
- **Market — no follow-up hit**: without an industrialized script and
  audience-feedback loop, one breakout is followed by several failures and
  cash flow breaks.
- **Concentration — platform policy risk**: heavy dependence on one
  short-video ecosystem, exposed to changes in external-link policy, a higher
  revenue cut, throttling of AI-labelled content or account bans.
- **Team, governance & regulation — infringement and takedown**: character
  faces alleged to infringe a real celebrity's likeness, training sets
  containing unlicensed film material, or scripts alleged to be plagiarized,
  bringing collective litigation and removal across platforms.

## Red flags
- Refusing to disclose the portfolio's average payback rate, and annualizing
  revenue from the single best-performing title.
- Fast GMV growth with acquisition spend persistently above 70-80% of revenue
  and free cash flow persistently negative.
- Striking, highly dynamic demo footage while real long-sequence delivery
  shows frequent face deformation and flicker, patched by outsourced manual
  repainting.
- Core revenue resting entirely on a third-party mini-program or a single
  account, with no owned app, domain or first-party user asset.
- A defective rights chain, with no proper end-to-end adaptation and
  distribution licences for the underlying web-fiction IP.

## Valuation models and exit norms
- **Valuation anchors**:
  - **Tooling / software (Track A)**: benchmarked against creator-economy and
    design-tool SaaS (Figma, Canva, Unity) on EV / forward ARR of 6-12x,
    conditional on software gross margin above 65% and stable NRR.
  - **Studio (Track B)**: benchmarked against high-technology animation and
    visual-effects companies on EV / EBITDA of 8-14x, or discounted
    per-project cash flow.
  - **Platform / distribution (Track C)**: benchmarked against mobile game
    publishers, web-fiction export platforms and streaming, strictly on
    **EV / net revenue after user acquisition** or P/E (10-18x). Never credit
    a premium to gross revenue before acquisition spend is deducted.
- **Exit paths**:
  - **Strategic M&A**: the buyers are the video traffic platforms (ByteDance,
    Kuaishou, Netflix), the large game publishers (for the cutscene and asset
    generation pipeline), or traditional media incumbents seeking a digital
    transition.
  - **IPO**: a platform or full-stack label needs net income stable at
    $30-50M and evidence of compounding industrial output across more than
    one title cycle.
